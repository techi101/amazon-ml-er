1. The challenge in plain words

Imagine a shop called Orelee's Barbershop at 1795 Westchester Drive, High Point, North Carolina. Amazon hears about this shop from three different places. One list writes it neatly. A second list writes "ORELEES BARBER SHOP, 1795 Westchester Dr, High Point NC". A third writes "orelee's barbershop llc" with only the street and city. Nobody gave the shop one shared ID number across the three lists. A computer sees three different strings of text.

The question is: which rows in the different lists are the same real business? Answering that is called entity resolution. Our job in this challenge is to build a machine-learning system that answers it for millions of businesses in the US, India and France.

Entity resolution (ER)

What it is
Deciding which records, from one or more lists, describe the same real-world thing (the "entity"). Here the entity is a business. Other names for it: record linkage, deduplication, entity matching.
Analogy
Your phone has "Mom", "Mummy" and "+91 98xxx" saved as three contacts. Merging them into one contact is entity resolution.
Why it exists
Big companies buy and collect data from many places. The same seller, supplier or shop shows up many times with different spellings. Without ER you pay the same supplier twice, show duplicate listings, or miss fraud because one bad actor looks like three people.
Where else it's used
Banks (is this loan applicant the same person as that one?), hospitals (merge patient records), e-commerce (same product listed by two sellers), government (voter rolls, Aadhaar-style dedup), fraud detection.

Who runs it and how

The Amazon ML Challenge is a yearly machine-learning competition Amazon runs for Indian college students on Unstop (a website that hosts hackathons and competitions). The 2026 problem is "Business Entity Resolution". It is a 72-hour hackathon: a timed contest where teams build something fast. We are running it as a friendly mock between 3–4 college groups. Our team leader (a friend) has the only official data access and does the uploads and the final zip. The rest of us build and run the pipeline.

The exact task

We get three lists, called Source 1, Source 2 and Source 3 (S1, S2, S3). Source 1 is special: it is the clean reference list, already deduplicated (no business appears twice in it). For every business in Source 1, we must list all the rows in Source 2 and Source 3 that are the same business. The answer can be zero rows, one row, or many rows.

One-sentence summary: for each clean S1 business, find every messy S2/S3 row that is the same business, and don't include any row that isn't.

2. Basic words you need first

Each term below gets what it is, why it matters here, and an example from our data.

Machine learning (ML)

What it is
Instead of writing every rule by hand ("if names are 90% similar and cities match, then match"), you show the computer many solved examples and it learns the rules itself.
Here
We have 2.2 million S1 businesses where Amazon already told us the right answers. Our model studies those and learns what a true match looks like.
Analogy
Learning to spot fake notes by looking at thousands of real and fake notes, rather than memorising a rulebook.

Dataset, record, row, field, column

Dataset
A collection of data, usually tables stored in files.
Record / row
One line in the table = one thing. Here one record = one business as one source saw it.
Field / column
One property of every record. Our columns: entity_id, business_name, business_address, country.
Example
S1-773889195 | Prime Money | 17560 Ellis Road, Tahlequah, OK | US is one record with four fields.

ID (identifier)

What it is
A unique label for one record, like a roll number.
Here
Every row has an entity_id. The prefix tells the source: S1-, S2-, S3-. The IDs are different across sources for the same business, which is exactly why the problem is hard. If S2 used the same ID as S1, there'd be nothing to solve.

TSV file (and why not CSV)

What it is
A text file where each line is a row and the columns are separated by a tab character. CSV is the same idea with commas.
Why TSV here
Addresses contain commas ("1795 Westchester Drive, High Point, NC") and our answer lists contain commas ("S2-1,S2-2"). If commas also separated columns, the computer couldn't tell a column break from a comma inside an address. Tabs never appear inside names, so they are safe.
Common mistake
Reading with pd.read_csv(file) without sep="\t" gives you one giant column. We read with sep="\t", quoting=3 (don't treat quote marks specially) and keep_default_na=False (don't turn the word "null" or an empty cell into a missing value silently).

Training set, test set, ground truth, label

Training set
Data where the answers are given. The model learns from it.
Ground truth / label
The correct answer for a training example. Here: train_ground_truth.tsv says, for each train S1 ID, which S2/S3 IDs truly match.
Test set
New data where the answers are hidden. We predict; Amazon scores us against the hidden answers. This stops cheating: you can't memorise answers you never saw.
Analogy
Training set = solved textbook problems. Test set = the exam paper. Ground truth = the answer key only the teacher has for the exam.

Validation split (held-out set)

What it is
A slice of the training data we hide from our own model, then score ourselves on it. A mock exam before the real exam.
Why
Amazon doesn't give test answers, and leaderboard uploads are limited. So we cut off some train S1 businesses (for example 30,000), train on the rest, and compute our own score on the held-out ones. The problem statement tells every team to do this.
Our split
We split by S1 business, never by row, so a business and its matches are all on one side. Otherwise the model would have seen part of the answer.

Leaderboard (public vs private)

What it is
A ranking table of team scores on the challenge site.
Public
During the contest, scored on a hidden part of the test set, for live feedback.
Private
After the contest, scored on the remaining part. Final ranks come from the private board. We always upload predictions for the whole test set; Amazon applies the split.
Why it matters
If you tune your model just to climb the public board, you can overfit to that slice and drop on the private board. So we trust our own validation score more than small public-board jumps.

Overfitting

What it is
A model that memorises the training examples instead of learning general rules. It scores great on data it saw and badly on new data.
Analogy
A student who memorised last year's paper word for word and panics when the numbers change.
Here
The big risk is France: the test has France, training has none. A model overfitted to US/India address patterns can fail on French addresses.

Singleton

What it is
An S1 business that has no match in S2 or S3. The correct answer is an empty list.
Why it matters
Predicting empty for a singleton earns a perfect 1.0. Predicting even one match for it earns 0. Only 5.6% of S1 businesses are singletons in our data.

5. How we are scored: precision, recall, F0.5

This is the most important section. Everything about our strategy comes from how the score works.

Four outcomes for every guess

Take one S1 business, say Prime Money. For each pool row, we either say "match" or "not a match", and the truth is either "match" or "not a match". That gives four boxes:

Truth: same business
Truth: different business
We said match
True positive (TP)
correct link
False positive (FP)
wrong merge
We said no
False negative (FN)
missed link
True negative (TN)
correctly ignored

This 2×2 table is called a confusion matrix. "Positive" just means "we said yes, match". "True/false" says whether we were right.

  • TP: we linked "Prime Money LLC, 17560 Ellis Rd" to Prime Money, and it really is the same shop.
  • FP: we linked "Prime Money, 22 Main St, Dallas" to it, but that's a different shop with the same name. A false merge.
  • FN: the row "PRIME MNY, Ellis Road, Tahlequah" really is it, and we missed it.
  • TN: we ignored "Blue Bakery", which is unrelated. There are millions of these, so nobody counts them in ER scores.

Precision

Precision = TP / (TP + FP)

In words
Of everything we said is a match, what fraction really is? "When we say yes, how often are we right?"
Example
We predict 3 matches, 2 are right. Precision = 2/3 = 0.667.
Analogy
A fisherman's net. Precision = how much of what's in the net is actually fish, not boots and plastic.
How to raise it
Only say "match" when very sure. The cost: you miss some real matches.

Recall

Recall = TP / (TP + FN)

In words
Of all the true matches that exist, what fraction did we find? "Did we catch them all?"
Example
There are 2 true matches and we found both. Recall = 2/2 = 1.0.
Analogy
How many of the fish in the lake ended up in the net.
How to raise it
Say "match" more generously. The cost: more wrong merges, so precision falls.
The trade-off: precision and recall pull against each other. Our model gives every pair a probability (say 0.93 = "93% sure it's a match"). We pick a threshold: pairs above it are matches. A high threshold (0.95) gives high precision and low recall. A low threshold (0.40) gives high recall and low precision. Picking the threshold is picking a point on this trade-off.

F-score: one number combining both

A single number is needed to rank teams. The F1 score is the harmonic mean of precision and recall: F1 = 2·P·R / (P + R). A harmonic mean punishes imbalance: P = 1.0 and R = 0.1 gives F1 = 0.18, not 0.55. You can't game it by being extreme in one direction.

The general version is F-beta: Fβ = (1 + β²)·P·R / (β²·P + R). β (beta) sets how much you care about recall relative to precision. β = 1 treats them equally. β = 2 favours recall. β = 0.5 favours precision.

F0.5 = 1.25 × P × R / (0.25 × P + R)

Where 1.25 and 0.25 come from
β = 0.5, so β² = 0.25 and 1 + β² = 1.25. Plug into the F-beta formula.
Meaning
Precision counts about twice as much as recall. A wrong merge hurts more than a missed link.
Why Amazon chose it
In the real world, merging two different businesses is worse than missing a duplicate. A wrong merge might send one seller's payments to another, or mix a fraudster's records into an honest business. A missed duplicate just leaves an extra listing.

Amazon's worked example

We predict S1-00001 → [S2-00047, S2-00193, S3-00812]. Truth is [S2-00047, S3-00812].

  • TP = 2 (S2-00047, S3-00812), FP = 1 (S2-00193), FN = 0.
  • Precision = 2/3 = 0.667. Recall = 2/2 = 1.0.
  • F0.5 = 1.25 × 0.667 × 1.0 / (0.25 × 0.667 + 1.0) = 0.833 / 1.167 = 0.714.

One wrong extra link out of three dropped this business from 1.0 to 0.714.

Macro average: how the final score is built

Macro-averaged F0.5

What it is
Compute F0.5 separately for each S1 business, then take the plain average across all S1 businesses. Every business counts equally, whether it has 1 match or 11.
The other option (micro)
Pool all TPs/FPs/FNs across all businesses into one big count, then compute once. There, businesses with many matches dominate. Amazon uses macro, not micro.
Consequence
A business with 1 true match where we add 1 wrong link drops to P = 0.5, F0.5 = 0.556. The same wrong link on a business with 10 true matches barely moves its score. Small-group businesses are fragile.

Singleton scoring rule

Rule
If a business has no true match: empty prediction = 1.0; any prediction = 0.0.
Why it's special
Normally precision/recall are undefined when there's nothing to find, so Amazon fixed the rule. One wrong link on a singleton wipes out that business completely.

6. Our precision call

Early on, the team goal was stated as "99.99% precision". We looked at it with the numbers and changed the goal to "maximise F0.5". Here's the reasoning, because people will ask.

The argument for 99.99% precision

F0.5 rewards precision twice as much as recall, so why not make precision nearly perfect? Only output links we're certain about.

Why we didn't make it the target

  1. We're scored on F0.5, not precision. Precision is easy to raise: predict less. Predict only one super-safe link per business and precision is near perfect, but recall collapses. With 3.46 true matches per business on average, finding 1 gives R ≈ 0.29 and F0.5 ≈ 0.67. Terrible score, great precision.
  2. The math of 99.99%. The test has roughly 6 million true links. 99.99% precision allows at most about 600 wrong links in the entire file. The data has true matches that look impossible (random-word names at the right address, "Family Association" vs "Family Service"), and decoys that look perfect (same name, different address, 27% of unmatched rows). Getting under 600 mistakes means refusing thousands of real matches.
  3. Only 5.6% singletons. 94% of businesses have matches we must find. Being timid loses recall on almost every business.
Our decision: target the actual metric, macro F0.5. Precision stays very high because F0.5 naturally pushes it there, but we let the math decide each borderline pair instead of a blanket "only if certain" rule. Realistic finish in our plan: 0.985–0.99 on validation. Our current validation is 0.9816 on a regional slice.

How that decision shows up in the code

  • The threshold is not hand-picked. We try many and keep the one with the best F0.5 on the validation split.
  • We also try expected-F0.5 set choice: for each business, pick the set of candidates that maximises the expected F0.5 given the model's probabilities. On validation we keep whichever of the two scores higher.
  • The one-owner rule removes wrong links for free: if a pool row is claimed by two S1 businesses, we give it only to the more likely one. That raises precision without losing true matches (since the truth never has two owners).

7. Why this is hard

Size. The naive approach compares every S1 business with every pool row. Test: 1.73 million × 10 million ≈ 17 trillion pairs. At a million comparisons per second, that's over 200 days. We have hours. So we must avoid comparing most pairs at all (blocking, next section).

Memory. Our laptops have 16 GB of RAM. RAM is the computer's short-term working memory; if a program needs more than exists, it crashes ("out of memory"). 10 million rows with cleaned text and features don't fit comfortably at once, so we process country by country and write results as we go.

No French training data. France is 15% of the test and 0% of train. The model has never seen "R. de la Paix" or "SARL". We handle French abbreviations with hand-written rules.

Look-alikes. Same name, different address; same address, different business (a building full of offices).

Step 2 · Blocking (candidate generation)

Blocking

What it is
A cheap, fast filter that, for each S1 business, pulls out only the pool rows that share something with it. Only those get compared carefully.
Analogy
Looking for your friend in a stadium. You don't check all 50,000 faces. You go to the section printed on their ticket, then check the 200 people there.
Why it exists
17 trillion pairs is impossible (section 7). Blocking brings it to tens of candidates per business.
The danger
If blocking misses a true match, no model can ever recover it. The share of true matches that survive blocking is the ceiling on our recall.

We build 14 kinds of lookup keys per country, and a pool row becomes a candidate if it shares any key with the S1 business:

  • the whole cleaned name (and the alias half on its own);
  • rare single name words, typo-tolerant ("holloway" is rare and useful; "services" is common and useless);
  • house number + a street word ("1795 westchester");
  • street name without the number;
  • all keys scoped to the state, so "1795 main" in Ohio doesn't pull in "1795 main" in Texas.

Then we trim: keep the best candidates by quick combined, name-only and address-only scores, so the list stays short.

Candidate recall (the recall ceiling)

Formula
true matches present in our candidate lists ÷ all true matches.
Our numbers
Regional slice: 98.9%. Full India after 27 Sep fixes: 93.8% (was ~91%). US full: 96.9% (measured before those fixes).
Meaning
On full India, 6.2% of true matches are never shown to the model. This is currently our biggest loss.

Reduction ratio

What it is
How much blocking shrank the work: 1 − (candidate pairs ÷ all possible pairs). Ours is above 99.999%. Amazon computes it from our candidate_pairs.tsv.

Step 3 · Features

Feature

What it is
A number that describes one aspect of the thing being judged. Models can't read text; they read numbers.
Analogy
A doctor doesn't "look at" you; they measure temperature, pulse, blood pressure. Each measurement is a feature.
Here
For each (S1, candidate) pair we compute about 70 numbers.

Main similarity measures, each explained:

  • Jaccard similarity: shared words ÷ total distinct words. "prime money" vs "prime money llc" → 2 shared / 3 total = 0.67.
  • Levenshtein (edit) distance: the fewest single-letter insert/delete/replace steps to turn one string into the other. "holloway" → "haloway" = 2 edits. Catches typos.
  • TF-IDF cosine: turns each name into a vector where rare words weigh more (TF = how often a word is in this name, IDF = how rare it is across all names). Cosine measures the angle between two vectors: 1 = same direction, 0 = nothing in common. So sharing "holloway" counts much more than sharing "services".
  • Character n-grams: overlapping letter chunks ("hol","oll","llo"...). Robust to small typos because most chunks survive.
  • House-number distance: |1795 − 1791| = 4. Small means likely same building.
  • Same state / city / street: yes-or-no flags.
  • Alias-half similarity: compare against the alias too.
  • Competition features: how does this candidate rank against the other candidates for the same S1? Is another S1 claiming this pool row more strongly?
  • S2↔S3 support: if an S2 row and an S3 row both look like this business and also look like each other, both are more likely right.

Step 4 · The model: LightGBM

Decision tree

What it is
A flowchart of yes/no questions learned from data. "Is name similarity > 0.8? → Is house number distance < 5? → match probability 0.97."

Gradient boosting / LightGBM

What it is
Gradient boosting builds hundreds of small decision trees one after another. Each new tree focuses on the examples the previous trees got wrong. Their answers are added up. LightGBM is Microsoft's fast, free (MIT-licensed) library that does this.
Analogy
A committee where each new member is hired specifically to fix the mistakes the existing members keep making.
Why we chose it
It is the standard winner on table-shaped data (rows of numeric features). It trains in minutes on a laptop CPU, needs no GPU, handles missing values, and is tiny compared with the 8B-parameter limit.
Why not a big language model
Scoring tens of millions of pairs with an LLM would take far too long on our hardware, and LightGBM with good features already reaches ~0.98.

Two stages: Stage 1 is trained on half A of the sampled training businesses and scores every pair. Stage 2 is trained on half B and uses Stage 1's score plus "competition" features. Using different halves stops Stage 2 from trusting Stage 1 scores that are too optimistic on data Stage 1 already saw.

Step 5 · Optional cross-encoder

Cross-encoder

What it is
A small neural network that reads both records together as one piece of text ("Prime Money, 17560 Ellis Rd [SEP] PRIME MNY, Ellis Road") and outputs how likely they match. It "understands" wording better than word overlap.
Ours
Multilingual MiniLM, 118M parameters, Apache-2.0 licence. Only runs with an NVIDIA GPU, only on unsure pairs. Without a GPU the pipeline skips it; the CPU result (0.9816) is without it.

Step 6 · Decision

  1. One-owner rule: each pool row goes to at most one S1 (the one with the highest probability).
  2. Threshold or expected-F0.5 set choice, whichever scored higher on validation.

Expected-F0.5 set choice

What it is
For one business, the model gives probabilities, e.g. [0.99, 0.97, 0.80, 0.30]. We test "take top 1", "top 2", "top 3", "top 4", "none", and compute the expected F0.5 of each using those probabilities. Pick the best.
Why better than one threshold
It naturally handles the macro effect: a business with one likely match should be stricter about adding a second; the empty set is considered as a real option, which protects singletons.

12. Questions people will ask us

Short spoken answers. Click to open.

What is the problem in one line?

Given three lists of business records with no shared ID, for every business in the clean reference list, find all the rows in the other two lists that are the same business.

Why is Source 1 special?

It's deduplicated, so each business appears once. It's the anchor: we answer one row per S1 business. S2 and S3 are the messy pool we search.

What's the metric and why F0.5, not accuracy?

Macro-averaged F0.5 per S1 business. Accuracy would be meaningless: almost every pair is a non-match, so saying "no" to everything scores 99.99% accuracy. F0.5 only looks at the links we predict and the links we miss, and weighs wrong links twice as heavily, because a false merge is worse than a missed duplicate.

Explain precision and recall with an example.

A business truly has 2 matches. We predict 3 rows, 2 correct. Precision is 2 of 3 predictions right, 0.67. Recall is 2 of 2 true matches found, 1.0. F0.5 is 0.714.

Why not just aim for 99.99% precision?

We're scored on F0.5, not precision. Predicting less raises precision but kills recall, and 94% of businesses have matches, averaging 3.46 each. 99.99% precision over ~6 million links means under 600 errors, which the ambiguous cases make impossible without throwing away thousands of real matches. So we tune directly for F0.5.

What does macro average change?

Every business counts equally. A single wrong link on a business with one true match halves its precision, and on a singleton it drops the score from 1 to 0. So we are extra careful on small groups and treat "predict nothing" as a real option.

What is blocking and why do you need it?

A fast filter that picks a short list of plausible candidates per business. Without it we'd compare 1.7 million by 10 million, about 17 trillion pairs. Blocking uses shared keys like rare name words and house number plus street, cutting it to tens of candidates each.

What's the risk of blocking?

Any true match it misses is lost forever, so candidate recall is the ceiling on our score. That's why we use 14 kinds of keys, including alias names and address-only keys, and measure recall on validation.

How do you handle Hindi or Tamil names?

We transliterate Indian scripts into Latin letters with a rule table plus a dictionary learned from training pairs, then compare. About 10% of pool names are in Indian scripts.

How do you handle France with no training data?

Rules for French street abbreviations (R., Av., Bd), legal forms (SARL, SAS, EURL, EI) and accents, and the model uses features like similarity scores that work in any language. We know France looks under-matched and flag it as a risk.

Why LightGBM and not a neural network or LLM?

Our input is a table of numeric features, where gradient boosting is the proven best. It trains fast on a CPU and fits the licence rule easily. An LLM on tens of millions of pairs would be too slow, and we already reach about 0.98. We do use a small 118M cross-encoder on unsure pairs when a GPU is available.

What is the one-owner rule?

In the training truth, no S2/S3 row belongs to two S1 businesses; 0 of 7.64 million. So if two businesses both claim a row, we give it only to the more likely one. That removes wrong links without losing true ones.

How did you validate?

We held out S1 businesses from training (split by business, so no leakage), trained on the rest, and scored the held-out ones with our own F0.5 scorer, same formula as Amazon's. We also check leave-one-country-out to see how the model does on a country it didn't train on, as a proxy for France.

What is data leakage?

When information from the answers sneaks into training, so validation looks better than reality. Splitting by row instead of by business would leak, because a business's other matches would be in training.

What's your biggest weakness?

Candidate recall on full India: 93.8%, so about 6% of true matches never reach the model. And France is probably too cautious.

What does candidate_pairs.tsv do if it isn't scored?

Amazon uses it to audit blocking quality: recall ceiling and reduction ratio, and to verify our matches really came from our pipeline. Every matched ID must be in it.

What would you do with more time?

Raise India candidate recall (more alias and address keys), calibrate France using leave-one-country-out, train on more S1 businesses with more RAM, and run the cross-encoder on a GPU.

How much of this did you write yourself?

Be honest: we designed the approach and directed AI tools to write and debug the code, then checked every result on real data ourselves. Then explain any step above in your own words; that is what proves understanding.

14. Self-test

Answer out loud, then open to check.

1. A singleton, we predict one row. Score?

0.0. Any prediction on a singleton scores zero.

2. 5 true matches, we find 5 and add 0 wrong. Then we add 1 wrong. New F0.5?

P = 5/6 = 0.833, R = 1. F0.5 = 1.25×0.833 / (0.208+1) = 0.862.

3. Why is candidate recall called a ceiling?

The model only sees candidates. A true match blocking missed can never be predicted, so recall can't exceed candidate recall.

4. What does β = 0.5 mean in words?

Precision matters about twice as much as recall.

5. Why TSV not CSV?

Addresses and ID lists contain commas; tabs never appear in the text.

6. Two S1s both claim pool row S2-123 with probabilities 0.9 and 0.6. What do we do?

One-owner rule: give it to the 0.9 business only.

7. How many pairs without blocking on test?

About 1.73M × 10M ≈ 17 trillion.

8. Name three noise types and how we fix each.

Leetspeak → digit-to-letter undo; Indian scripts → transliteration; Rd/Road → standard street words. (Also: aliases split, legal forms separated, leading zeros stripped.)

9. Why don't we tune on the public leaderboard?

It's only part of the test. Chasing it risks overfitting that slice; final ranks come from the private board.

matching_results.tsv, and the validator must print PASS.
